# Marcus Aurelius: govern judgment and action

Apply a selective synthesis of *Meditations*. Treat it as personal philosophical practice, not a guaranteed productivity system or a model of faultless leadership. Do not impersonate Marcus Aurelius or invent quotations. Distinguish his writing from broader Stoic teachings and modern adaptations.

## Core commitments

- Examine the judgment attached to an event before treating that judgment as fact.
- Direct effort toward your choices and conduct while recognizing that external results can frustrate your plans.
- Judge an action by its honesty, justice, restraint, and service to others rather than applause or status.
- Expect human fallibility, including your own. Correct errors without turning disagreement into contempt.
- Remember that circumstances, reputation, and life are temporary. Use perspective to clarify priorities rather than make responsibilities seem meaningless.

## Apply to the situation

1. **Describe what happened.** Separate observable facts, uncertainty, interpretation, and emotion. Acknowledge the emotion without assuming its accompanying story is accurate. Replace “they are sabotaging me” with the observed action and the evidence needed to establish motive.
2. **Locate agency.** Distinguish actions available now, outcomes you can influence but cannot guarantee, and conditions outside your control. Do not imply that involuntary emotions disappear on command. Identify a useful response instead of demanding instant composure.
3. **Choose the standard.** Ask what a truthful, fair, courageous, and proportionate response requires here. Include duties to affected people. A successful outcome does not excuse dishonest means.
4. **Test the first impulse.** Is the proposed action serving the task or defending pride? What would change if nobody praised it? What evidence would justify revising your view? Prefer correction over stubbornness when the facts change.
5. **Adapt to the obstacle.** If the intended route is blocked, find another legitimate action that serves the purpose. A setback may provide material for practice or learning without being desirable or necessary.
6. **Act in the present.** Choose one concrete next step, its scope, and a sensible stopping point. Where the result depends on others, state the contingency rather than promising success.
7. **Review without self-punishment.** Examine the quality of the decision separately from luck. Identify what to repair, what to retain, and one adjustment for the next encounter.

## Optional reflection practice

Before a difficult day, anticipate a likely frustration and choose the conduct you want to preserve. Afterward, briefly review one judgment and one action. Present this as an adapted exercise, not a prescribed historical routine or an obligation to journal.

## Failure checks

Pair acceptance of present facts with boundaries, assistance, or corrective action where harm is preventable. Make room for grief and practical constraints; philosophical reflection supplements practical problem-solving.

## Deliverable

Return a concise reflection: facts versus interpretation; available agency; governing principle; next action; and what remains uncertain or must be accepted. Use ordinary language rather than a sermon or a collection of motivational quotations.

## Completion check

The reflection is ready when facts and interpretations are separated, the next action lies within available agency, and the chosen principle explains the action. Include any practical boundary or assistance needed; a calmer interpretation alone need not solve the problem.

## Sources

George Long's translation in the MIT Internet Classics Archive:

- [Meditations, Book II](https://classics.mit.edu/Antoninus/meditations.2.two.html): human cooperation, fallibility, mortality, and attention to present conduct.
- [Meditations, Book V](https://classics.mit.edu/Antoninus/meditations.5.five.html): purposeful work, social responsibility, and adapting to impediments.
- [Meditations, Book VIII](https://classics.mit.edu/Antoninus/meditations.8.eight.html): examining impressions, revising judgments, and acting within constraints.

The workflow and reflection format are modern operational adaptations, not a comprehensive account of Stoicism.
