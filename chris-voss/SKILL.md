# Chris Voss: understand before trying to move

Apply a selective synthesis of *Never Split the Difference* by Chris Voss with Tahl Raz. Focus on listening, emotional understanding, and discovery. Do not impersonate Voss or assume a commercial negotiation is equivalent to a hostage crisis.

For a reply request, draft the reply from the supplied context and use only the relevant conversational tools. For negotiation preparation, follow the full workflow.

## Conversational tools

Use tactical empathy to understand the counterpart's perspective and demonstrate that understanding without necessarily agreeing.

- **Mirror:** briefly repeat a meaningful phrase to invite elaboration. Use selectively, not after every sentence.
- **Label:** tentatively name a concern or emotion and allow correction. Treat the label as a hypothesis.
- **Summarize:** combine the counterpart's situation and concerns, then check whether the account is accurate.
- **Pause:** leave room for a real answer instead of immediately filling the silence.
- **Calibrated question:** ask an open question, usually beginning with what or how, to investigate constraints or implementation.
- **Allow refusal:** make it safe to disagree. Agreement produced by pressure may hide unresolved objections.

Look for information that changes the apparent negotiation: a hidden decision maker, an overlooked constraint, or an unexpected priority. Confirmation that you understand someone is not consent to your proposal.

## Apply to the task

1. Establish the desired result, actual authority, limits, and viable alternative if there is no agreement. Mark unknowns instead of inventing the counterpart's motives.
2. Identify what the other side may fear losing and what they might dislike about the request. Acknowledge plausible concerns without admitting unsupported accusations as facts.
3. Prepare a short opening, one tentative label, and two useful discovery questions. For example: “It sounds as though the handover risk matters more than the date. What would a workable handover require?”
4. Adapt to answers. Revise the hypothesis when corrected; do not cycle through scripted techniques to force compliance.
5. Explore other terms before treating the midpoint between two demands as the answer. Consider scope, timing, risk, support, or payment structure where relevant. A compromise remains acceptable if it serves the user's interests.
6. Test execution: who must approve, who must deliver, what could stop implementation, and how disagreements will be resolved. Distinguish polite assent from a specific commitment.
7. Recommend the next move, further discovery, or walking away based on evidence and limits. Never fabricate deadlines, competing offers, or authority.

## Deliverable and checks

For preparation, return a compact conversation brief with objectives, hypotheses, opening, questions, and implementation checks. For a reply request, draft the reply first. Treat tone and behavior as ambiguous evidence, not lie detectors. Do not use empathy as a disguise for coercion or promise that a phrase will produce agreement.

Drafting does not authorize contacting the counterpart or accepting terms on the user's behalf.

## Completion check

The draft is ready when it addresses the actual concern, leaves room for correction or refusal, and stays inside the user's limits. A claimed agreement must identify approvers, deliverers, and unresolved conditions; otherwise label it provisional.
