# Weekly status update: make progress and needs easy to see

Adapt Will Larson's practice in “Sending weekly 5-15 updates”: aim for roughly fifteen minutes to prepare an update that can be read in five. Keep a lightweight record during the week, then edit it into a useful account of progress and priorities.

The practice helps others understand the writer's focus, connects related work across teams, and preserves evidence for later reflection and reviews. It can reduce routine status recitation during one-on-ones. Treat the time budget as a convenience, not a requirement to pad a short update or skip essential verification.

## Build the update

1. **Set scope.** Establish the reporting period, audience, and relevant commitments. Reuse an existing team format when supplied. For a manager, emphasize decisions and confidence in delivery; for reports, include context, priority changes, and actions leadership owes them.
2. **Gather bounded evidence.** Use the supplied notes and relevant authorized records for that period. Check previous commitments where available. Avoid broad searches through unrelated communications just to fill the report.
3. **Separate states.** Distinguish completed, in progress, planned, and blocked work. A merged change is not necessarily deployed; a deployment is not evidence of customer impact. State the strongest result the evidence actually supports.
4. **Select what matters.** Lead with meaningful changes, missed commitments, emerging risks, and decisions. Tie activity to a result or learning where known. Omit routine detail that changes no understanding or action.
5. **Make requests actionable.** For an issue needing help, state the impact, requested decision or support, responsible person when known, and needed-by date. Include options or a recommendation if supported. Do not invent an owner, deadline, or level of urgency.
6. **Check the account.** Verify dates, numbers, links, attribution, and status. Preserve uncertainty and acknowledge contributors. If evidence is missing, ask for the essential fact or mark the draft clearly; do not fabricate progress.

## Suggested format

Use only the sections that carry information:

- **Summary:** the main change and overall delivery position, supported by evidence.
- **Results and learning:** a few consequential outcomes, with links where useful.
- **Risks and decisions:** impact, request, owner, and timing.
- **Next priorities:** the intended near-term work and its purpose.

Prefer a short paragraph or a few bullets per section. If the recipient must act, place that request near the top. Use status colors only when the team has defined what they mean. Separate a forecast from a commitment and avoid presenting hoped-for benefits as measured results.

## Quality checks

For example, replace “Worked on onboarding” with a verified result such as “Completed the onboarding prototype; user validation is still pending.” Do not add an improvement percentage without measurements.

Urgent issues should not wait for a weekly reporting cycle: flag the need for timely escalation. Prepare a draft without sending or publishing it unless the user authorizes that action. Keep sensitive personnel information out of a broadly distributed update.

## Completion check

The draft is ready when consequential claims match the supplied evidence and reporting period, missed commitments remain visible, and the reader can identify changes, next priorities, and requests without opening every link. Label missing owners or dates instead of supplying them.
