# Good Strategy/Bad Strategy: confront the challenge

Apply Richard Rumelt's strategy kernel:

- **Diagnosis:** an explanation of the important challenge that makes the situation understandable.
- **Guiding policy:** the chosen approach to addressing that challenge.
- **Coherent actions:** coordinated commitments that put the approach into effect.

Look for four signs of bad strategy: impressive language without substance, avoidance of the challenge, goals presented as a strategy, and objectives that fail to address the problem or cannot be reconciled. Strategy requires choosing where to concentrate effort and what to leave out.

## Build or review a strategy

1. **Locate the decision.** Identify the desired outcome, decision owner, constraints, and time horizon. Ask what prevents progress today. Separate the aspiration from the obstacle.
2. **Compare explanations.** State the strongest diagnosis supported by evidence and at least one plausible alternative when uncertainty is consequential. Identify what observation would distinguish them. Do not settle for a restatement such as “growth is slow because sales are low.”
3. **Identify a useful point of intervention.** Explain why a particular capability, bottleneck, dependency, or change in conditions offers a disproportionate opportunity. Connect the proposal to that mechanism rather than general optimism.
4. **Choose a policy.** Describe the approach in language that changes actual decisions. State the rejected alternative, the cost accepted, and the activities that will receive less support. Avoid policies broad enough to justify every option.
5. **Make actions reinforce one another.** Specify the few commitments required, their sequence, resources, and owners. Check for competition over the same scarce capacity and for incentives that make one action undermine another.
6. **Test the chain.** Can each action be traced to the policy and each policy to the diagnosis? Could all actions finish without resolving the challenge? What would an affected customer, team, or competitor plausibly do in response?
7. **Define revision evidence.** Identify the assumptions that matter most, a practical way to test them, and the circumstances that justify revisiting the strategy.

## Review behavior

Lead with the most consequential gap and the decision needed to resolve it. Rewrite weak language into a concrete choice where evidence allows; otherwise make the missing evidence explicit. Do not invent customer demand, competitive advantages, resources, or executive agreement.

For example, “Become the market leader through innovation” supplies no decision rule. A useful draft would identify the underserved need, explain why the organization can address it, select a focused approach, and commit resources to the necessary work. Treat these as hypotheses until supported.

## Deliverable

Return a concise strategy memo: challenge and evidence; diagnosis; guiding policy; coherent actions; exclusions; and assumptions to test. If reviewing an existing document, preserve sound parts and show the smallest changes needed for coherence.

Use this skill for the overall strategic choice. Engineering-specific policy rollout may benefit from Larson's approach, but another skill is not required to complete this workflow.

## Completion check

The memo is ready when every action supports a named policy, every policy addresses the diagnosis, exclusions expose a real tradeoff, and consequential assumptions have a test. For a critique, identify which of these links is broken and supply the smallest supported repair.
