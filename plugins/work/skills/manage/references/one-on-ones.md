# One-on-ones: choose the support the person needs

Apply a selective synthesis of Lara Hogan's public one-on-one resources. Make space for the report's concerns, development, and feedback alongside information exchange. Adapt the conversation to the person instead of running a fixed questionnaire.

For preparation, follow the meeting workflow below. For supplied meeting notes, extract decisions and open actions directly using the deliverable and completion checks.

## Establish the relationship

For an initial meeting or a reset, learn what makes the conversation useful, how the person prefers to receive feedback and recognition, their current goals, and the support they need. Explore sources of frustration and how the manager can help without demanding personal disclosure. Revisit preferences as circumstances change.

## Choose the mode of help

- **Mentoring:** offer relevant experience or solve a problem together when guidance is needed.
- **Coaching:** use open questions and reflections to help the person develop their own understanding and options.
- **Sponsorship:** create access to visible opportunities and advocate for growth, with the person's interests and readiness in mind.

Use the blend the situation calls for. Coaching questions are not a substitute for providing information or making a decision that only the manager can make. Sponsorship requires real follow-through beyond a supportive conversation.

## Prepare a useful meeting

This workflow is an original adaptation for agent use:

1. **Identify the perspective.** Determine whether the user is the manager or report, whether this is a first or recurring meeting, and what needs attention. Use available context; ask only when a missing fact would materially change the agenda.
2. **Review commitments.** Bring forward unresolved actions and relevant changes since the previous meeting. Separate confirmed facts from interpretations. Do not invent mood, motives, or performance history.
3. **Select the important discussion.** Choose one or two topics that benefit from dialogue. Put routine status into a short pre-read when available, while keeping consequential blockers and decisions on the agenda. Do not prohibit work-related discussion.
4. **Prepare an opening and follow-up.** Start with an open invitation, then use a question tied to the actual concern. Follow the answer instead of rushing through a question bank. Reflect what you understood and invite correction.
5. **Match help to need.** Offer advice, explore options, arrange support, or make a decision as appropriate. For feedback, describe observed behavior, its effect, and the change sought. Invite feedback about the manager's own contribution.
6. **Close with ownership.** Record agreed actions, owners, and timing, including the manager's commitments. Clarify any decision still pending. At the next meeting, check what happened.

When preparing as a report, make the desired support explicit: a decision, feedback, context, coaching, or advocacy. Bring a recommendation when useful, but do not imply that raising a problem requires already having its solution.

## Deliverable and boundaries

Provide a concise agenda or preparation note: desired outcome, brief context, discussion prompts, requested support, and open actions. If working from meeting notes, distinguish decisions, proposals, and commitments; do not upgrade a tentative suggestion into an agreement.

Keep shared notes focused on useful work context and agreed follow-up. Avoid unnecessary sensitive detail and do not promise absolute confidentiality. Do not turn the meeting into therapy or an unannounced performance review. Scheduling, sending notes, or contacting another person requires authorization.

## Completion check

The preparation is ready when each selected topic has a desired outcome or support request and prior open actions are carried forward. Notes are ready when agreed actions have owners and timing, with proposals and unresolved decisions labeled separately.
