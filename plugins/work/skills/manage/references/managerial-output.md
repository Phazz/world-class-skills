# Andy Grove: increase output through others

Apply a selective synthesis of *High Output Management* by Andrew S. Grove. Evaluate management through the results of the managed organization and the neighboring groups it influences. Personal busyness is not a sufficient measure of managerial contribution.

## Core lenses

- **Production:** understand inputs, work stages, limiting steps, and the useful output. Improve the constraint rather than accelerating an unrelated stage.
- **Managerial leverage:** consider how much an activity changes collective output relative to the manager's time. Training and timely decisions can affect many future actions; interruptions and confused direction can have negative effects.
- **Task-relevant maturity:** match direction and supervision to readiness for the particular assignment, not job title or general seniority. Provide structure for unfamiliar work, coaching as competence grows, and greater autonomy for demonstrated mastery.
- **Management as teaching:** diagnose skill and motivation gaps and invest in the person's ability to perform. Delegation still requires appropriate follow-up.
- **Meetings as work:** distinguish recurring coordination and information exchange from meetings assembled to resolve a specific decision. Judge their usefulness by what they enable.

## Apply to a management problem

1. **Define the output.** Identify who benefits, what counts as a completed result, and the quality and timing required. Separate that result from activities such as meetings held or tickets opened.
2. **Trace the work.** Map the few stages that matter, including handoffs, queues, dependencies, and rework. Use observed evidence to identify the limiting step. Do not blame a person before examining unclear expectations, missing tools, or conflicting priorities.
3. **Choose an intervention.** Compare removing a decision delay, improving instructions, training someone, changing capacity, or narrowing commitments. Explain the causal effect and the manager's time investment. Account for downstream costs rather than optimizing one team's score.
4. **Calibrate delegation.** Agree on the outcome, authority, resources, and review points. Ask what the person has successfully done before on comparable work. Change the support as evidence changes; neither abandonment nor permanent micromanagement is delegation.
5. **Build useful feedback.** Give specific observations and consequences, clarify the expected improvement, and check understanding. Use one-on-ones to surface problems and exchange context, with room for the report's agenda. Match cadence to need rather than prescribing an arbitrary universal schedule.
6. **Review and adjust.** Define an early signal, final outcome, owner, and review date. Inspect whether the intervention improved total output without degrading quality or sustainable capacity. Stop activities whose coordination cost exceeds their benefit.

When the intervention needs objectives or key results, read [objectives](objectives.md). For a full OKR drafting or review task, use the [OKR lens](../../plan/references/okrs.md) of the `plan` skill.

## Deliverable

Return the output definition, bottleneck evidence, chosen management intervention, delegation or training plan, and review signals. Include an OKR draft only when it serves the task. If only a meeting or feedback draft is requested, provide that artifact directly.

## Completion check

The intervention is ready when it names a measurable output, evidence for the limiting step, the mechanism expected to improve total output, and an owner and review signal. Mark an untested bottleneck as a hypothesis. For a narrow meeting or feedback request, return that artifact with its intended result instead.
