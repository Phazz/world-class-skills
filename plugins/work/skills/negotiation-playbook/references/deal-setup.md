# 3-D Negotiation: shape the conditions for agreement

Apply a selective synthesis of *3-D Negotiation* by David A. Lax and James K. Sebenius. Diagnose three dimensions rather than treating every obstacle as a communication failure:

- **Tactics:** how people communicate and bargain at the table.
- **Deal design:** how terms, interests, and differences can be combined to create a workable agreement.
- **Setup:** which parties and issues are involved, their alternatives, and the sequence and process that bring them together.

A skillful conversation cannot reliably rescue a deal whose structure or participants make agreement unattractive. Consider changing those conditions before making further concessions.

## Map the situation

Use this original worksheet to turn the framework into a decision:

1. State the desired outcome, current impasse, and available evidence. Identify what is an observed barrier and what is only a hypothesis.
2. Map parties beyond the visible negotiators: approvers, implementers, affected groups, potential partners, and veto holders. Record each party's interests, authority, alternatives, and dependencies. Mark unknowns explicitly.
3. Explain each party's no-deal path. Compare the current proposal with that path from their perspective, not just the user's. Ask who benefits from delay or the status quo.
4. Diagnose whether the main obstacle is tactical, structural, a setup problem, or a combination. Tie the diagnosis to a concrete fact.

## Design a better path

Compare a small number of plausible moves:

- **Tactical move:** resolve a misunderstanding or improve the exchange of information.
- **Design move:** change the bundle of terms, distribute risk differently, or make a commitment conditional on an observable event. Identify measurement and enforcement difficulties.
- **Setup move:** involve a missing decision maker, expand or narrow the issues, develop a legitimate alternative, or change the sequence of discussions.

For each move, explain who becomes more willing to agree, why, what new objections appear, and what it costs. Adding parties can increase value and also create vetoes or coordination overhead; more participation is not automatically better.

Work backward from an implementable agreement to the preceding decisions it requires. Specify who must be aligned before the next discussion and which commitments should remain conditional. Do not assume you control other parties' alternatives or can recruit a coalition merely by naming it.

## Stress-test the proposal

Check that every necessary participant has a reason to accept, the signatories have authority, and implementers can deliver. Test the path if a pivotal party refuses, a timeline slips, or an alternative disappears. Avoid commitments that destroy the user's viable alternatives before reciprocal obligations are secured.

Use legitimate incentives and accurate information. A stakeholder map does not authorize contacting people, disclosing confidential information, or creating commitments.

## Deliverable

Return the stakeholder map, bottleneck diagnosis, comparison of possible moves, recommended sequence, and next uncertainty to resolve. For example, repeated discount requests may reflect an absent budget approver; another price concession would not fix that setup problem.

## Completion check

The brief is ready when each necessary party has an interest, approval role, and no-deal path recorded or marked unknown; each proposed move names whose incentive changes; and the recommended sequence survives a pivotal refusal or identifies the fallback.
